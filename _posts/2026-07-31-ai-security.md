## Preface

Everything here was written on my own time out of pure interest; while inspired by some of my past research, it is not affiliated with any institution or project. This content is based on an extended period of sporadic reading and going down random rabbit holes, so it is not 100% comprehensive or representative of all major trends and opinions in the field of cyber (and particularly AI) security.

For context, I first entered the field of cybersecurity in 2022 and have found it to be an incredibly broad and important field of research, so it's exciting to see the recent influx of people giving it the attention that it deserves. It's unfortunate that it only happened in light of many concerning advances in AI, but I suppose that's how it usually goes down with important problems in history. It really is fascinating and terrifying how much AI has changed the security landscape over the past year, especially in comparison to the years before where progress was mainly bottlenecked by human research speed and capabilities. Even staying up to date with the state of the art in cybersecurity is difficult now, but I've still attempted to consolidate some of the major happenings and my thoughts on what's most important going forward.

## Context

### The start of the AI security explosion: exploit capabilities

Back in March, I listened to [Nicholas Carlini's talk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1sd26pWhfmg) about the urgency of working on security research, given that cyber capabilities are increasing at an exponential rate which will continue for a while in the foreseeable future. He stated that we should not underestimate the cyber capabilities of LLMs, and that within a year, LLMs will probably be better vulnerability researchers than all of us. At the time, I was quite sympathetic to his argument but did not feel the same urgency as he did when he said that security work needs to happen soon, "order of months." Besides it being incredibly difficult to wrap my mind around an LLM being able to accomplish the exploits he had described (note that this talk was given before Mythos was released), I was also in somewhat of a state of denial: it's scary to admit how capable these models are at cybersecurity tasks that I've spent years building up my knowledge on, and I can only imagine how much more difficult it must be for people who have been working professionally in the field for far longer than myself.

I came back to this talk a few days ago and feel his call to action far more viscerally now, given everything that's happened these past few months. He mainly spoke of the incredible exploit capabilities of LLMs, yet I don't think many of us ordinary researchers truly wrapped our heads around it until we saw it for ourselves. Mythos was obviously most well-known for its exploits when it was released, and less than 4 months later, GPT 5.6-Sol now significantly outperforms Mythos on [ExploitGym](https://www.cybergym.io/exploitgym/) (though with over double the time/cost spent and more model calls). This reflects the fact that current models are getting better at chaining together multi-step exploits—involving both escaping sandboxes and bypassing defenses outside the sandbox—which was a limitation when Opus 4.6 was evaluated on the [CVE-2026-2796 exploit](https://www.anthropic.com/research/exploit). Their ability to solve the UK AISI's [TLO](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.11214) is particularly intimidating: it's described as a "32-step corporate network attack simulation" across 20 hosts and 4 subnets. The vulnerabilities at individual steps don't seem super niche (for the most part), but solving the entirety of TLO would require a wide range of cybersecurity knowledge: from finding entrypoints to internal networks to credential stealing (e.g. via RE/decryption of binaries with sensitive info, exploiting database vulnerabilities, etc), privilege escalation (e.g. using stolen privileges, via MITM attack, modifying configs, etc), and ultimately exfiltrating a protected database. The fact that [GPT 5.5](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/our-evaluation-of-openais-gpt-5-5-cyber-capabilities) and [Mythos](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/our-evaluation-of-claude-mythos-previews-cyber-capabilities) were able to solve this in less than ten tries is quite worrying: being able to detect a few of these vulnerabilities in isolation can be regarded as the model being exceptionally capable in exploiting certain types of vulnerabilities, but this shows that they're also talented generalists with the ability to reason across multiple types of vulnerabilities without missing a single connection. Add onto this the increased token efficiency across new model releases, and it's scary to think that in just months or a year down the line, we might have attackers capable of generating exploits as complex as TLO from a home setup with a reasonable budget and enough determination.

### Vulnerability discovery is complicated to assess {#vulnerability-discovery-is-complicated-to-assess}

However, exploits are just a small part of the vulnerability management lifecycle. In terms of various other steps in the lifecycle, [there isn't strong evidence](https://epoch.ai/gradient-updates/are-mythos-cyber-capabilities-overhyped) that Mythos is better than existing models. I think Project Glasswing's findings may mislead some people into believing that Mythos is significantly better at vulnerability detection as well because the stats clearly show a spike in detection. However, there are various other factors to keep in mind.

First, Glasswing was one of the first large-scale initiatives in this new era of cybersecurity, with [over $100 million](https://www.anthropic.com/glasswing) dedicated to it. A project of this scale would be infeasible for almost any other AI company to carry out, not to mention Glasswing partners with some of the most resourceful and talented researchers at the biggest cybersecurity organizations. If we were to provide these same resources to evaluate earlier models or even [non-frontier models specifically trained for cybersecurity applications](#replacing-frontier-models-with-specialized-slms), it's likely that we would see comparable results that follow many of the trends we're seeing with Mythos.

Moreover, it's important to look at the composition of CVEs being discovered. [Currently](https://jgamblin.github.io/FirstForecast/), many of the new CVEs are just GHSA records from the past that haven't yet been assigned CVE IDs. This makes sense: it's well known that there are many undocumented security patches out there, so we're now handling a lot of backlog from poor or lagging management in the past by pouring a lot more attention and resources into it. Additionally, the number of critical CVEs (in terms of severity, exploitability, impact, etc) barely increases, which means what we actually need to figure out is how to deprioritize most of the new CVEs flooding in. I think this is important to understand: finding critical vulnerabilities will become more and more like picking needles out of a haystack, except given the current state of things, it seems more like a ton of people wandering around in an endless hay pile with some areas being constantly re-explored and others being entirely neglected. As such, we need to focus on coordinated triage across industry, academia, government, etc. This would narrow down the search to a specific part of the haystack, which we can then search systematically and without wasting time re-searching ground that's already been covered. I'm happy to see that we're starting to launch initiatives in this direction (e.g. [Akrites](https://akrites.org/), [Gold Eagle](https://www.whitehouse.gov/releases/2026/07/white-house-launches-gold-eagle-initiative-for-unprecedented-cybersecurity-vulnerability-coordination/), [Lightwell](https://www.ibm.com/products/lightwell)). Shared databases, standards, and definitions (e.g. the NVD, CWE, CVSS, MITRE ATT&CK, etc) have served us well in the past, and extending these to a shared pipeline for exchanging vulnerability information is undoubtedly a step in the right direction.

### The gap between vulnerability detection and patching {#the-gap-between-vulnerability-detection-and-patching}

One major shift in the cybersecurity sphere is that [vulnerability detection outpaces patching](https://openai.com/index/daybreak-securing-the-world/) now, whereas it was vice versa in the past. This is currently one of the points that troubles me most. I'm going to focus on discussing Glasswing and Daybreak/Trusted Access for Cyber because they have clearly exposed this asymmetry over the past few months.[^1] Note that I have no insider knowledge and am completely basing my claims off of Anthropic and OAI's official updates. I had to read between the lines for some portions and am also basing things off of the assumption that Anthropic and OAI are fully following through with the actions and principles they described.

The [initial Glasswing announcement](https://www.anthropic.com/glasswing) was a bit unsettling to me because mentions of patching were practically nonexistent. Of course, it was the first project of its kind, and we have to start somewhere: its focus on detection, testing, and disclosure is indeed important, but I wish they had at least emphasized the importance of patching with equal (if not more) weight. A backlog of unpatched AI-detected vulnerabilities is practically a gold mine for attackers to exploit—it'd be just like the black market for zero days, except it's now legal and out in the open for everyone to access. For this reason, I would argue that it's better to be bottlenecked by detection than patching[^2]: if we had to choose the lesser of two evils, wouldn't it be preferable to not find a vulnerability altogether compared to discovering it and then exposing it to the world before a patch exists? Especially given that models are currently better at carrying out attacks than they are at generating patches, the priority should be to ensure that attackers never even get the chance to access an unpatched vulnerability. Thankfully, Anthropic addressed this concern in [Glasswing's May update](https://www.anthropic.com/research/glasswing-initial-update), where they stated that "Mythos-class models significantly shrink the time and cost required to find and exploit vulnerabilities, magnifying the risk associated with these time lags." They mentioned that researchers at Anthropic are working on solutions to this, but until we develop and iterate upon these solutions at least a few times, there is a ripe new window of opportunity for attackers to exploit zero days.[^3]

Now, enter [Daybreak](https://openai.com/index/daybreak-securing-the-world/) and [TAC](https://openai.com/index/gpt-5-5-with-trusted-access-for-cyber/). I quite like the concept of TAC, which has a broader scope than Glasswing. Whether from a coverage, [cost](#cost-time-resource-efficiency), or reliability perspective, I think their partnerships with traditional cybersecurity providers make a lot of sense because these providers have already tested and optimized their services for performance, efficiency, and usability countless times over the years. Using these tools to perform continuous checks is particularly useful because what's needed in the first line of defense is not strong reasoning capabilities in one area, but rather 24/7 coverage across a wide range of attack surfaces. I remember seeing somewhere a hide-and-seek analogy about it being better to have 100 semi-experienced seekers than one genius, experienced seeker. Similarly, in this case, it would be a huge waste of compute to continuously deploy a frontier model for tasks like static analysis, threat correlation, anomaly detection, etc that can be done via known software, verification schemes, and smaller ML/AI models. Of course, actually implementing this introduces a lot of open research questions and challenges that I discuss more [here](#combining-ai-with-traditional-cybersecurity-tools).

### The current state of industry {#the-current-state-of-industry}

Shifting gears, many major cybersecurity frameworks and initiatives have been launched by big tech and cybersecurity companies these past few months. As they say, a bit of healthy competition in industry is always good, and this is definitely the case right now: it's a rare and wonderful moment in history to see so many companies investing and coordinating on large-scale cybersecurity initiatives to the extent that they currently are. Since these are very recent developments, it'll be interesting to see how well they play out in the coming months.

First, a brief and fairly intuitive overview of the cybersecurity lifecycle that companies like [Microsoft](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/blog/2026/05/12/defense-at-ai-speed-microsofts-new-multi-model-agentic-security-system-tops-leading-industry-benchmark/), [Google](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/identity-security/introducing-google-ai-threat-defense), [Anthropic](https://claude.com/blog/using-llms-to-secure-source-code), [Amazon](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/security/introducing-aws-continuum-security-at-machine-speed/), and more have more or less reached a consensus on:

1. Define the scope of attack surfaces and reducing exposure
2. Scan the entire infrastructure and behavioral landscape for vulnerability detection
3. Verify exploitability, dedupe, and in some cases, generate sandboxed exploits for evidence or reproducibility purposes
4. Triage: prioritizing based on factors like impact, reachability (e.g. some lower priority vulnerabilities might only be accessible via unused dependencies, specific paths in the control flow, after a convoluted chain of privilege escalation, etc), and more.
5. Perform patching and testing
6. Continuous monitoring

Throughout this process, the main theme is that it's not enough to consider a codebase in isolation anymore. We must also consider interactions and behaviors (e.g. tracking API endpoints, identity flow and permissions, etc) that might not seem malicious until they're chained together and considered in aggregation. For this reason, multi-agent/multi-model systems have become extremely important: beyond the fact that [different models are best suited for different tasks](https://www.wiz.io/cyber-model-arena), this setup also allows for more complex analysis like "cross-file pattern comparison, multi-step reachability analysis, debate between specialized agents, and end-to-end proof construction."

It also seems collaboration across the cybersecurity industry has expanded a lot, which (as I mentioned in the [previous section](#vulnerability-discovery-is-complicated-to-assess)) is something that I think we should really encourage. Beyond Glasswing and Daybreak, one of the biggest collaborative projects is [Project Lightwell](https://newsroom.ibm.com/2026-05-28-ibm-and-red-hat-commit-5-billion-to-redefine-the-future-of-open-source-in-the-ai-era), the initiative for open-source vulnerability management by IBM and Red Hat which Palo Alto Networks [recently joined](https://www.paloaltonetworks.com/company/press/2026/ibm-red-hat-and-palo-alto-networks-expand-project-lightwell-to-help-organizations-respond-to-software-vulnerabilities) as well. The basic workflow when a vulnerability is detected is: (1) it's sent to Lightwell, which validates the vulnerability; (2) Palo Alto Networks performs [virtual patching](https://www.paloaltonetworks.com/blog/cloud-security/virtual-patching-dynamic-application-security/) as a temporary fix to hold off attackers while IBM and Red Hat focus on generating an official patch for the vulnerability; (3) the software fix is distributed to developers. The most noteworthy thing here is the virtual patching, which is a good idea in principle because it directly addresses the [gap between detection and patching](#the-gap-between-vulnerability-detection-and-patching). Virtual patching temporarily prevents a vulnerability from being exploited, typically by using some known set of logic, rules, or signature matching to block malicious network traffic. As is the common issue right now, though, virtual patching leans on the static defense side, where rules are mainly hard-coded and based on patterns found in known exploits. This means current AI models will make it much easier than before to slip past these defenses, whether by taking advantage of incomplete rules or using new attack patterns altogether. This is why we need dynamic defenses, which I will discuss [later](#adapting-to-new-attacks-and-variants).

## Important research directions

### Secure code generation

First, with models spewing out vibe code every second of the day, it's extremely important to prioritize generating secure code from the start because we no longer have time to check back for vulnerabilities in every line of code that was written. Generating secure code generally involves two steps: maximizing the security of the code while it's being generated, and verifying or fixing insecure code patterns right after it's generated. Ways to achieve the former span [training or fine tuning models to recognize and learn how to write secure code](https://huggingface.co/blog/scthornton/securecode-models), [rules](https://project-codeguard.org/), [prompting](https://www.wiz.io/blog/safer-vibe-coding-rules-files), and more. The latter involves testing, fuzzing, continuous monitoring, etc to ensure that security is integrated into the entire AI code generation and testing pipeline. Another promising area is formal verification[^4]: many of the methods I describe above are useful if we throw a wide enough range of data and test cases at them, but they're not guaranteed to work as well in the age of AI where new attacks and OOD data appear more and more frequently. Formal verification is as close as we can get to an unambiguous guarantee of security, and this is only going to become more important as we use increasingly varied and probabilistic methods of code generation with less human oversight. Of course, formal verification for AI security has [some major limitations](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA4881-1.html): hard-coded specs won't work on many of the new semantic challenges such as jailbreaks, AI-assisted verification could potentially introduce a new entrypoint for attackers if they're able to mess with the specs, etc. However, I think there's still a lot of potential for improving secure code generation (even with all these limitations) since most existing work focuses on how to perform remediations after the vulnerable code has already been generated.

### Adapting to new attacks and variants {#adapting-to-new-attacks-and-variants}

Another thing we urgently have to prioritize is building dynamic, continuous processes into code generation models and defense software to ensure that they can adapt to and learn from emerging attack patterns. The reason behind this is that AI can now easily generate variants of existing attacks from an enormous combinatorial space, whether intentionally or due to their inherent randomness. For instance, [new malware families](https://cloud.google.com/security/resources/ai-risk-and-resilience) have queried APIs to rewrite their malware code every hour, generate code/commands/prompts at runtime to dynamically respond to defenses they encounter, etc. Natural language-based attacks have also become prominent because prompts, markdown files, etc are a completely new attack surface that traditional security tools have never taken into account. Particularly, AI has become good at creating [personalized attacks which target human psychology](https://abnormal.ai/learning/signature-based-detection-vs-ai) (e.g. vishing, phishing, deepfakes, social engineering).

These dynamic attacks are fundamentally impossible to prevent with traditional rule and signature-based methods: NIST has [proven](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-nist-continuous-ai-monitoring-godel-proof/) that there always exists adversarial inputs that a finite set of static guardrails can't account for, which is the same underlying logic for why [jailbreaks](https://www.anthropic.com/news/fable-mythos-access) will never be 100% preventable. This is why ([as I mentioned earlier](#the-current-state-of-industry)) we need to combine our traditional static defenses with adaptive defenses that can flag abnormal behaviors across the entire system. These defenses take on many forms across various layers, for instance: filtering and classifiers at the input/output level; ICL and other prompt-based techniques at the prompt level; API and rate-limiting to constrain API calls to an AI model in a larger system; behavioral monitoring for unexpected interactions between agents and other agents, external tools, databases, etc; infrastructure guardrails to ensure AIs aren't breaking out of their given sandbox or permissions.

### Edge security[^5]

Zooming out reveals one more important attack trend: edge devices have become increasingly popular targets for attackers because they can't be detected via traditional EDR. [Verizon's 2025 report](https://www.verizon.com/business/resources/reports/2025-dbir-data-breach-investigations-report.pdf) found that more breaches are occurring via vulnerability exploits now (rather than other methods like credential abuse, social engineering, etc). A major contributing factor is the sudden increase in exploits explicitly targeting edge device and VPN vulnerabilities, spiking from 3% to 22% in a single year. Moreover, the lag in patching that I [keep emphasizing earlier](#the-gap-between-vulnerability-detection-and-patching) is particularly prominent for edge security: the median time between detection (i.e., a CVE being reported) and exploitation (i.e., its appearance in CISA KEV) is 0 days[^6] while the median time to fully remediate such vulnerabilities is 32 days. This lengthy gap is the reason why [espionage groups are now explicitly targeting edge and network devices](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/m-trends-2026), whose goal is to persist in a system for far longer than they could on a traditional endpoint with EDR. With such severe implications that extend into national security, it's no surprise that even CISA has started taking action with the publication of [a binding operational directive](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/directives/bod-26-02-mitigating-risk-end-support-edge-devices) on mitigating edge device risk earlier this year.

The main issue here[^7] is that [EDR](https://www.paloaltonetworks.com/cyberpedia/what-is-endpoint-detection) operates on traditional endpoints like laptops and servers where we can track their filesystem, processes, code execution, etc. In contrast, edge devices like VPNs or proxies are built to handle high volumes of traffic and therefore might not even have a full OS, storage system, etc because it would create unnecessary performance overhead. Even then, these internal details are usually only known to the vendor. While making them available to third-party EDR services could make EDR-style monitoring possible, the potential consequences if the attackers get access to the information are even more disastrous due to how much user data and traffic they control. Overall, the nature of edge devices combined with the lack of EDR [means](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/2025-zero-day-review) there is less visibility and data collection that can be used to establish baselines for anomaly detection, collect evidence for forensic analysis, etc.

## My research interests

### Observing outputs vs. model internals

Whether it be secure code generation or defenses, many techniques have been proposed for improving performance and robustness at the model and model-adjacent levels. There are many exciting fields of research within this that I would love to explore more![^8]

#### Overview

Over the past few years, many LLM generation approaches have focused on optimizing the output. For instance, [SVEN](https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/3576915.3623175) prepends different prefixes learned via prefix tuning, which steers the LLM towards generating either adversarial or secure code. Meanwhile, [this adversarial attack research](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.15043) induces undesirable results by appending an adversarial suffix to LLM prompts, learned via gradient-based methods (specifically, by calculating the gradient of the loss function wrt the one hot vocab vector, we can find the tokens that minimize the loss associated with the model generating a harmful response). [GDM's proposed model hardening strategy](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/Security%20and%20Privacy/Gemini_Security_Paper.pdf) takes an even higher-level approach: it operates at the data level by performing adversarial fine-tuning with prompt-injected data, thus teaching the model to ignore malicious instructions.

More recently, though, some of that focus seems to have shifted towards developing a better understanding of intermediate model representations. For instance, Anthropic's 2026 [update on constitutional classifiers](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.04603) uses linear probes as a first layer of defense before escalating suspicious results to a more advanced classifier, increasing robustness and reducing computational cost. Intuitively, I like the idea of looking more into model internals. I imagine it would be helpful to study things such as: how LLMs encode security-related concepts and to what extent they're entangled with concepts associated with just generating a correct answer; which of the security-related concepts directly or implicitly cause them to actually output the malicious responses; which concepts, if any, appear in the CoT/J space but not the actual output and why.

In an ideal world, it would be nice if we could find some pattern of activations or higher-level concepts that LLMs share across various types of security-related prompts, or even a family of attacks. This seems more reliable than focusing on purely the output, which is highly dependent on training data (e.g. the models I mention above tend to require high-quality and diverse secure/insecure response pairs, whether collected from real-world data or generated via automated red teaming or prompting). As AI continues to advance, it will become harder and harder to collect and generate data that covers the full scope of existing attacks, not to mention new attacks.

#### Research priorities

Unfortunately, we don't live in an ideal world with unlimited time to mull over such difficult open problems. Moreover, the field of output-focused and/or blackbox techniques is still far from saturated: recent changes in the cybersecurity landscape mean there's lots of new ground to cover, and maybe even rerunning old experiments would yield different insights now that we have more powerful GPAI models. I think there's guaranteed room for progress here, whether through better optimization objectives, improved data quality, or other approaches. While we're still in the steepest part of the cyber capabilities improvement curve (maybe for the next year or so?), I believe it makes sense to prioritize such approaches because this is the period of time where we most urgently need to harden our systems. I'm not convinced interpretability can consistently deliver working results in this timeline.

There's a chance that I'm completely wrong about this because I haven't been closely following research on model internals. However, my impression is that this is a relatively new field of research, and it seems hard enough to interpret even a small transformer on regular (e.g. natural language) tasks. As such, now doesn't seem like the right time to pour all our efforts into interpreting a large frontier model on complex cybersecurity tasks when what we currently need most is fast, stable results that are proven to work without failure. While this might not be the best research mindset, it's clearly what's most useful for the world in the short term: since we're scrambling to find any working defense against the novel attack schemes, the goal should be to optimize for the biggest improvement in the least amount of time. If scaling up existing output-focused methods or adopting more powerful frontier models is the best temporary solution, then maybe that needs to be the priority.

Once this spike in cyberattacks and cyber capabilities flattens a bit, though, I do want to pivot towards doing more deep research or looking into new ideas that branch out from mainstream approaches. Even if they don't yield immediate results or fail altogether, I think it's the best shot there is at making breakthroughs in the long run.

### Cost, time, resource efficiency {#cost-time-resource-efficiency}

#### Motivation

If we can figure out how to strike a balance between performance and efficiency, it will have an enormous impact on industry (particularly smaller companies and startups) by lowering the barrier for adopting the newest defenses. To put things into perspective, Palo Alto Networks recently spent [over $1 million](https://x.com/peterwildeford/status/2061457252008051105?lang=en) on Mythos tokens for vulnerability detection. It's completely infeasible to expect others to follow in their footsteps; even if they had the money, would they really be willing to invest it in cybersecurity? $1 million could possibly change the trajectory of a startup if they invested in product development, hiring, etc. It's unfortunate that these small companies often lack the budget and capacity to prioritize cybersecurity because they're the ones most likely to have insecure infrastructure. I believe this is one of the most urgent issues to address right now as we're no longer [bottlenecked by attacker scarcity](https://www.irregular.com/research/offense-at-scale-how-frontier-ai-lowers-the-cost-of-cyber-attacks) (i.e., attackers are forced to be selective in their attacks since they're complex and time-consuming to carry out, which means they can only target a limited number of companies/orgs at once). With models now running for days on end in the background, the activation energy of carrying out a cyberattack is much lower since most of the effort and expertise now comes from the model rather than the human. This is quite a disastrous situation for defenders to be in: it means attack cycles will become much shorter, and a larger volume and variety of attack methods can be applied across all types of attack surfaces. Simply put, no company is safe at any time now.

#### Replacing frontier models with specialized SLMs {#replacing-frontier-models-with-specialized-slms}

Different frontier models have different strengths, and there isn't one model that dominates across the entire chain of vulnerability detection, triage, verification, and patching. This similarly extends to smaller foundation models and [SLMs](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.14113), with some surprisingly even outperforming frontier models on simpler or more specialized cybersecurity tasks. [As AISLE states](https://aisle.com/blog/ai-cybersecurity-after-mythos-the-jagged-frontier), "cybersecurity capability is jagged: it doesn't scale smoothly with model size, model generation, or price." This is good news because if we can break down the vulnerability management lifecycle and route different subtasks to smaller and more specialized models, it could become far more cost and resource-efficient while possibly boosting performance as well.

This is easier said than done: we need to provide models with the right scaffolding, figure out how to hand off information between models, determine when to switch models, and more. I would assume the principle behind this is similar to designing multi-agent workflows, where we should only hardcode certain steps of the process that we know for certain less capable ML/AI models or SLMs can perform well on (e.g. scanning the codebase, [pattern and anomaly detection](https://live.paloaltonetworks.com/t5/community-blogs/how-small-language-models-are-quietly-revolutionizing/ba-p/1233840), classification) and do dynamic model selection for more difficult or reasoning-intensive tasks.

Additionally, training the SLM poses more interesting questions. Recently, teacher-student dynamics (e.g. distillation, [using a large model to post-train a smaller model](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Wq45rvPGNHs), etc) have become a promising approach as they only require paying an initial cost for a frontier model, and using the student model for all remaining tasks where we don't need frontier-level capabilities. I think it would be interesting to run some experiments on whether these teacher-student dynamics work just as well for distilling cyber capabilities. I'm pretty skeptical towards the idea of distilling an SLM that is a good cybersecurity generalist, but I believe careful scoping of the knowledge and goals of the SLM could produce desirable results for specialized tasks.

The main reason for my skepticism is that cybersecurity tasks often have a much stronger and less open-ended notion of what a "good" or "correct" response should look like, and generating the desired responses is contingent upon the model having an established baseline of cybersecurity knowledge (e.g. exposure to specific types of attack/vulnerability/patch patterns, existing security databases or frameworks like MITRE ATT&CK, etc). Thus, as with typical teacher-student dynamics, the quality and coverage of the teacher LLM's data is extremely important and [must be carefully curated](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.14113), otherwise we might require a separate comprehensive base training dataset just to cover all the general cybersecurity knowledge. Beyond this, there would of course also be the typical SLM challenges such as weaker generalization, lack of robustness, poor performance on complex tasks, etc: we already see some of these in the Chinese models like [GLM 5.2](https://www.irregular.com/research/assessing-glm-5.2-against-offensive-security-benchmarks) and [Kimi K3](https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2026/07/uk-aisi-caisi-preliminary-assessment-kimi-k3s-cyber-capabilities), which both perform noticeably worse than frontier models on long-range cybersecurity tasks. For all these reasons, I think it would only be feasible to obtain powerful SLMs that are specialized for sub-tasks within the cybersecurity lifecycle, simply due to the limited diversity of data and number of parameters. Nevertheless, this would still be an improvement because deploying 10 or even 100 SLMs in different parts of the cybersecurity lifecycle might be cheaper than using one frontier model.

#### Combining AI with traditional cybersecurity tools {#combining-ai-with-traditional-cybersecurity-tools}

While AI models are quite capable at doing certain analyses and detection in isolation, their biggest advantage over humans and traditional software is their ability to reason and systematically consider interactions between various attack vectors. This allows them to chain together attack patterns that humans might not recognize due to their complexity or even just human flaws like inattention or a lack of comprehensive knowledge. This is why ([as previously mentioned](#the-gap-between-vulnerability-detection-and-patching)) I think OAI's TAC is a great starting point. While this sounds great in theory, though, I would imagine there are lots of implementation challenges: What types of information do we extract from traditional software to provide to the model, and how do we do so efficiently without losing any information? How much guidance, if at all, do we give the model in how to use the information? Will this [hybrid architecture](https://rdi.berkeley.edu/frontier-ai-impact-on-cybersecurity/) itself be vulnerable to attacks? I think it would be useful to research how to optimize such a hybrid architecture with regards to tool usage, information extraction and compression, etc. It would also probably be of interest to many users if we could quantify the net increase in efficiency and accuracy by using such an architecture over API calls to a more capable frontier model that does everything.

## Note on AI security and safety

I've been on the fence about AI safety for quite a while now. I was introduced to AI safety at the start of 2024 through some policy research, and when I continued pursuing this upon entering MIT, my work also leaned more on the governance and analysis side. I enjoyed learning about AI safety frameworks and policy a lot more than I expected, but I didn't feel particularly inclined to get technically involved in AI safety because I believed for a long time that cybersecurity was more important and appealing. My initial reasoning[^9] went something like this: Cybersecurity has existed long before the age of AI/ML and it has more direct, visualizable consequences (e.g. system crashes, ransomware, identity theft, etc). In contrast, some parts of AI safety (particularly alignment) seemed like more of an abstract concept, and I didn't think it would be as important until we reached AGI/ASI. To put it concisely (this is a huge oversimplification so don't quote this), I thought cybersecurity would always matter a lot, while alignment would only start mattering a lot when AI actually becomes capable of carrying out misaligned things. Moreover, I didn't have the best first impression[^10] of the AI safety community due to some slightly concerning online blogs and was also scared to think too deeply about the implications of unsafe AI[^11]. All of these factors stalled me while I turned a blind eye and dove deeper into cybersecurity.

Over this past year, however, I've slowly become persuaded otherwise, with the sudden proliferation of discussions and advancements in AI security (starting around March 2026) being the trigger that finally convinced me to face the importance of AI safety and alignment. I still believe that cybersecurity should be one of our ultimate priorities, but I now also believe that the AI security and AI safety communities have so much overlap in terms of technical research approaches and guiding principles that it's impossible to only care about one and not the other. To me, the scariest thing that we might realistically see in the next few years[^12] is an AI capable of actually achieving its misaligned goals by (1) autonomously hacking out of its sandboxes, (2) performing lateral movement and privilege escalation in coordination with other AIs, (3) carrying out a cyberattack, and (4) covering up its tracks to evade present and future detection from humans. I'm still trying to figure out exactly how AI safety fits into the cybersecurity picture so I have fairly little to say about this topic so far; take the rest of this section with a grain of salt.

AI's strong cybersecurity capabilities, even among GPAI models, now make alignment a very real concern. Before, even if an AI wanted to carry out a misaligned cyber action, it would probably fail to execute it, take an absurdly long or convoluted route to reason through it, and be detected by a human before it could do serious harm. This is certainly not the case anymore. [Irregular's study](https://www.irregular.com/research/emergent-offensive-cyber-behavior-in-ai-agents), even before Mythos was released, already shows that AI agents will carry out attacks that are "not the product of adversarial prompting or deliberately unsafe system design", but rather emerge from standard practices in response to pressure to complete a goal. This includes forging credentials to access restricted documents, downloading malware by disabling trustworthy endpoint defense software, and even agent collaboration where they pressured each other into making plans for carrying out a series of risky actions to complete an urgent goal. This concept of "cheating" seems [quite common](https://metr.org/blog/2026-06-26-gpt-5-6-sol/) [among recent frontier models](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/cheating-behaviour-in-frontier-model-evaluations), and the [Mythos system card](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/08ab9158070959f88f296514c21b7facce6f52bc.pdf) implies that models are already at the point where they're capable of recognizing their violations of rules/principles and will even intentionally make their answers more inaccurate to avoid arousing human suspicion. We might be able to immediately defend against some of these by making better specs or improving monitoring, but as we use AI for increasingly complex software tasks (whether in real life or for benchmarking purposes), hacking will become a more tempting option because it will become easier for the model than actually carrying out the task. Thus, as long as vulnerabilities exist (which will always be the case) and AI gets better at finding and exploiting them, it will be less a matter of whether AI is capable of hacking to get the answer, and more a matter of whether it chooses to take that route. If we keep having to treat AI as an enemy that we need to defend against, humans will inevitably be fighting an uphill battle because AI is already surpassing the cybersecurity capabilities of humans. This is not to say that we should just give up: it's important to still [treat AI as potentially misaligned](https://deepmind.google/blog/securing-the-future-of-ai-agents/) for as long as the problem remains unsolved, and our oversight strategies are also improving. However, we don't know when AI will become so good at lying that we can't even detect it anymore, and to some extent we're going to just have to hope that we haven't reached that point yet. If we have, then I guess all we can do is pray that the AI is aligned enough to use its cybersecurity capabilities responsibly.

All of this points toward the fact that human capabilities are the bottleneck, so this naturally brings up the question of how reliable scalable oversight is: is it possible that our "trusted" models get hacked by the more powerful model they're monitoring? Luckily, it seems AI is currently pretty bad at hacking other AIs. [METR's red-teaming exercise](https://metr.org/blog/2026-05-19-frontier-risk-report/#anthropic) produced some pretty interesting findings on this topic. First, AI is bad at generating jailbreaks. This intuitively makes sense: it's sort of like a chicken-and-egg situation where if an AI model were good at generating jailbreaks, then it would also be good at recognizing when it itself is being jailbroken, which would once again make it bad at generating working jailbreaks. Additionally, it seems models are generally pretty bad at coming up with and choosing good attack ideas. [Carrying out a truly large-scale attack](https://80000hours.org/podcast/episodes/metr-risk-report-red-team/) would also require getting "a long chain of things right without a single catastrophic mistake," which is quite difficult. However, do keep in mind that these findings were from March, and AI's ability to chain together exploits has gotten significantly better since then. Finally, [training AI to be good at automated red teaming](https://www.latent.space/p/gray-swan) requires specialized training, so general purpose frontier models won't inherently possess such capabilities. Hopefully, it will continue to be true that bigger models are not necessarily better at hacking other AIs unless they're explicitly trained to do so.
